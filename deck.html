Five cards

You found the hidden deck. It isn’t in any menu, so either the stage page made you suspicious or you went digging in the console. Either way, welcome. :]

1.

The trick

Pick any five cards. My assistant looks at them, slips one into a pocket, and hands me the other four. From those four alone, I’ll name the card I never saw.

The assistant and the magician are two separate functions in this page’s source, and the magician only ever receives four cards, in order. View the source if you don’t believe me.

2.

How it works

Do the trick once, and this box will walk through your hand specifically.

Five cards, four suits. At least two of your cards share a suit; that’s the pigeonhole principle doing the heavy lifting. One card of that pair goes into the pocket. The other goes down first, so its suit is the hidden card’s suit.

A clock with thirteen hours. Put the ranks around a clock, ace through king. Walk clockwise from one card of the pair to the other and it takes some number of steps \( d \); walk from the other and it takes \( 13-d \). Those can’t both be 7 or more, so one direction is at most 6. The assistant pockets the card at the far end of the short walk, and the first card down is where the walk starts.

Three cards, six orders. That leaves three cards to say a number from 1 to 6, and three cards can be laid down in exactly \( 3! = 6 \) orders. Rank them low, middle, high (if two share a rank, break the tie by suit: ♣︎ < ♦︎ < ♥︎ < ♠︎), and the order spells the count:

low, mid, high1
low, high, mid2
mid, low, high3
mid, high, low4
high, low, mid5
high, mid, low6

That’s the whole thing. Five cards for four suits, thirteen ranks that never sit more than six steps apart, six orders for six counts. Every number in the trick is exactly as big as it needs to be, and not one bigger.

4.

Credit where it’s due

This trick isn’t mine. It’s one we learned at Ross in the summer of 2026, and it’s about as Ross as card magic gets: think deeply about simple things, and five ordinary cards turn into a code.

Its roots go back a lot further than my summer. It’s credited to William Fitch Cheney Jr., who was both a stage magician and the first person to earn a math PhD from MIT, in 1927. It first showed up in print in Wallace Lee’s Math Miracles in 1950, and Michael Kleber’s essay “The Best Card Trick” is still the best place to read about it.

Kleber also shows that with a cleverer code, five cards are enough to pull this off with a deck of 124. Finding that code yourself is harder than it sounds, and a lot more fun.

Like the Gilbreath shuffle on the stage page, the proof here is short enough to ruin the trick, which is still the best property a trick can have.
