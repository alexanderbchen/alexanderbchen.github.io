The memorized deck

I keep one deck in an order I know by heart: thirteen years, four suits, and every card exactly where I left it.

1.

The spread

Fifty-two cards, face down, in the order they happened. Hover to fan through them, tap one to deal it into the light, or turn the whole spread over and watch thirteen years land face up.

2.

Four suits

♠︎

Spades are the math, and they start late. The Ace doesn’t show up until sixth grade, and for a while after that it’s mostly practice. Then the suit comes in a rush: three AIMEs in three Februaries, a USAMO, a summer at Ross, and a King I didn’t see coming, a published paper.

♥︎

Hearts are the music, the oldest suit in the deck. Its Ace is a five-year-old at Carnegie Mellon, and its King is a 98 at Westmoreland. Somewhere in the middle, I joined the String Orchestra and learned to play inside a sound much bigger than mine.

♦︎

Diamonds are the water: ten years of black lines, two trips to Eastern Zones, back-to-back undefeated seasons, and relays where four of us share a single time.

♣︎

Clubs are the people, which feels right, since so many of them came from a club. Peter, who kept showing me harder problems. Ryan, who’s writing ARC with me. Family 5 at Ross. The kids in my classes at the Chinese school, and a math club that’s grown past eighty-five.

A suit has thirteen cards, and from my first piano lesson to this fall is thirteen years on the calendar, 2014 through 2026. I didn’t plan that. The deck did.

3.

Eight perfect shuffles

Of course, a deck in perfect order is practically begging to be shuffled.

A faro shuffle cuts the deck into two exact halves and weaves them back together, one card from each side, perfectly. It looks like chaos. Shuffle once and my first piano lesson lands right next to the fall I became Math Club president. Keep going and the years scatter across the table.

Shuffle eight times, though, and every card comes home.

The reason is arithmetic. Number the positions 0 through 51, and a perfect out-faro sends the card in position i to position 2i, counting modulo 51. Eight shuffles multiply by 28, and

28 = 256 = 5 × 51 + 1,

so every card lands exactly where it started. It’s the same kind of arithmetic I spent a summer at Ross rebuilding from the axioms, tucked inside a flourish.

Two cards never move at all: the first and the last, a five-year-old at the piano and a senior swim season that hasn’t started yet. Two more, my first WPIALs and SSAMO, just trade places, shuffle after shuffle. I didn’t plan that either.

4.

The jokers

Before a faro, a magician takes the jokers out. Mine sit at the edge of the table, but they’re the reason the rest of the deck holds together.

The black joker is me at thirteen, attempting an ace production on a triple lift. The red one is the Gilbreath principle, the day magic and math stopped being separate hobbies.

Since then, nearly everything I do runs through a card trick sooner or later: the research, the classes I teach, the essays I write. A joker can stand in for any card. That’s the job magic does in my life.

P.S. There’s a second deck hidden somewhere on this site, with a trick we learned at Ross. It isn’t on this page.

5.

The blank card

Magicians keep a blank card or two for the moments they can’t plan. Mine is 2027.

I haven’t written it yet, and it’s already my favorite card in the deck.
