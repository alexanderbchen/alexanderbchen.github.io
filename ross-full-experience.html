Ross, the full experience

Six weeks, one building, four people: one unforgettable experience.

← Back to the spoiler-free version

The actual spoiler

Spoiler alert. The other page keeps the mathematics to itself on purpose, and so does this one. Everything else is fair game: the routine, the building, the food, the nights, the people, how it ended. If you’re going to Ross and you’d rather walk in knowing nothing, go back.

The real spoiler isn’t anywhere in the problem sets. I went to Ohio for six weeks of number theory and came home with a girlfriend, which was not on the packing list I spent two weeks optimizing.

She was one of the four people I spent nearly every waking hour of that summer with, so for the first stretch she was mostly the person across the table at 2am telling me my proof was induction wearing a trench coat. She’s a better mathematician than she lets on in a first conversation. She’s also the one who found the room on the third floor with the stained glass, which became the good spot for the rest of the summer.

I went into Ross self-contained on purpose. Years of treating that as a strength: work alone, work faster, count the results at the end. What I didn’t expect from a math camp was to find out how much of that was just a way of keeping people at a distance I could measure. Six weeks with her and the other three, and the habit stopped being worth defending.

We’ve been long distance since the last day, which is its own skill and one I’m still bad at. The plan, as of now, is MIT together. Ask me in April how that went.

She’s in a lot of the pictures below. I’m not going to say which.

Move-in day

Otterbein University, Westerville, Ohio. June 14th to July 24th. My directions on arrival were a sheet of printer paper taped to the pavement with an arrow drawn on it, and I remember thinking that a program this famous for rigor could have splurged on a real sign.

The dorm was cinderblock, one long hallway, and a room with nothing in it. I made the bed badly. This was the first time in my life I’d lived away from my parents, and the first few hours were genuinely great: nobody to tell me when to sleep, when to eat, what to do next.

It took about a day for the other side of that to show up. Nobody told me when to sleep, and nobody told me which lecturers were kind, which ones cold-called, or which professor would keep you in a lobby until four in the morning. You worked all of it out yourself, usually the hard way, and usually while already behind on a problem set.

The day, on repeat

Every day had the same shape. Wake up, eat breakfast, go to lecture, solve problems, lunch, more problems, play Among Us or Wavelength with whoever was around, soccer in the evening, sneak out for ice cream, check in for curfew, sneak out again and sometimes get caught, sneak out one more time to do more problems, sleep. Then again.

The morning lecture was mandatory and it moved slowly on purpose. Afternoons were the opposite: optional advanced talks on distributions, group theory, elliptic curves, topology, representation theory, whatever a counselor felt like teaching. You could sit in on something you had no business understanding and nobody minded. I sat in on a lot of those.

The problem sets were the actual program. Everything else was scaffolding around them. You wrote proofs, your counselor read every line, and you found out how much of what you believed you could actually defend.

Twenty redos

Twenty redos across eighteen problems, first week. A redo means your counselor hands the proof back to be written again. Mine handed back nearly all of them, each one circled in red with the same question in the margin, in handwriting that never got tired of asking it: why?

I’d never received a redo in my life before that week. I’d also never had to prove that no integer sits strictly between 0 and 1, and it turns out I’d been assuming roughly forty things a page without noticing. My intuition was fine. My proofs were opinions with equations in them.

Kishen was my counselor, which at Ross means a college student a few years older who lives down the hall and reads everything you write. Other counselors let a good guess stand; he made me prove it, then made me sharpen it into something the next proof could stand on. Where everyone else wrote “close enough,” he wrote “explain in person” and waited. I’d seen him leave a “great job” and a smiley face on other people’s sets. Mine came back with neither, and for three weeks I was sure that meant he’d decided I wasn’t any good.

I had the sign backwards. He wrote me a letter at the end of the program and it read nothing like the margins of my problem sets: proud to have been my counselor, the only limits on me are the ones I put there, a whole paragraph about soccer and how I’d talked him into a sport he found boring. The redos were the attention. I’d spent six weeks reading them as a verdict.

Towers Hall

Almost all of it happened in one building. The stairwell was concrete and narrow and it twisted, so you never saw more than four steps ahead. Outside it the ceiling dripped whenever it rained, from somewhere I could never locate. In July a storm brought part of that ceiling down into the corridor. Someone set a cone beside the hole with signs warning about black mold, and we walked around it for the rest of the summer.

Towers 112 was the lecture room: wide, shallow, three blackboards running the front wall corner to corner. A Baldwin upright stood by the door with keys the color of weak tea and a dead A1, and I practiced a piece in A minor on it anyway, most days, badly. Chalk on slate is a dry sound you feel a little in your teeth. My handwriting has never been better than it was on those boards.

The boards got wiped three times a day. Morning class left half-arguments up, a lemma with a line through it, somebody’s guess in the corner with a question mark after it. By the end of a session all three boards were full of finished proofs with nothing crossed out, and then the next class came in and erased all of it. Towers Hall didn’t keep anything.

Up one floor there was a professor’s office that stayed unlocked all summer, and that room became ours. A sofa, and a table long enough for six that the four of us covered completely. We ate instant ramen in there with silverware stolen from the cafeteria, because the spoons vanished in week one and never came back. We taught each other in there. Cried in there, once over a problem set and once over an SAT score. Watched a show with a laptop propped on a stack of drafts and nobody moved the drafts first. Somebody found a room on the third floor with stained glass in it, and for a while that was the good spot.

Family 547

First-years get sorted into groups called families. Mine was Family 5, and I’ll say for the record that we were the top two families of all time. Family meetings ran in the evening and were supposed to be a check-in, which they were for about ten minutes before turning into topology, group and field theory, and eventually Galois theory on a whiteboard nobody had asked for.

Across both Families: 5 and 47, there were four people I ended up spending basically every waking hour with. Eighteen to twenty hours a day, for forty days. We did problem sets together, taught each other whatever we’d picked up in the afternoon talks, presented to each other, ate every meal together, and were roundly insufferable about it.

That group is the reason the math got better. Ross hands you a set of axioms and takes away everything else, and the fastest way through that is four people who will all tell you, flatly and repeatedly, that the step you like best doesn’t follow.

After curfew

Curfew was a check-in, not a lock. You checked in, waited a respectable amount of time, and went back out. Usually for ice cream, occasionally to sit on a curb and argue about a problem, once or twice to get caught, which was embarrassing for about a day.

The rest of the night was Wavelength at 1am, Among Us until someone got genuinely angry (ahem, including giving me a black eye), and problem sets. One professor caught a few of us in the main lobby and lectured for six or seven hours on quadratic reciprocity and Diophantine geometry, finishing around four in the morning. Nobody left. That’s the part I’d have found hardest to believe before I went: it wasn’t endurance, we just wanted to hear the end of it.

The Fourth fell in the middle of it. We walked out to a parking lot, watched the town’s fireworks over the trees, and were back on problem sets within the hour.

Everything we ate

Six weeks of a dining hall teaches you its schedule better than your own. The cookies came out warm at a specific hour and I took a plate of them, not a cookie, on principle. Birthdays got a cake carried across the whole hall. And every so often we escaped into town, which is how a Mexican place down the road ended up holding the title of worst date I planned all summer, by a review I did not get to write.

The Ross World Cup

The actual World Cup was on while we were there, so a lot of evenings were a crowd of mathematicians in a lounge shouting at a stream. That went to my head, and I organized one of our own: families merged into teams, a bracket, fixtures on the lawn in the evening heat.

My family combined with my friends’ family, and we won the first ever Ross World Cup. I scored a hat trick in the final, which I mention here because it’s my website. The whole thing lives over on the soccer page, trophy included.

The last week

Once the last problem set was in, the schedule loosened and people started giving talks. Some were serious, some were a slide that said BREAKING NEWS in a red box. I talked about my Anderson localization work, mostly the computational side, and it was the first time I’d presented that to a room of people who’d interrupt me.

Hearing everyone else was the better half. Six weeks of watching how these specific people thought, and then a week of watching how they explain, which is not the same skill and not always the same person.

Then the lawn photos, which took four tries because nobody could hold a pose, and the goodbye letters, which everybody at Ross writes and nobody warns you about.

One more thing

One of those four is my girlfriend now. She’s the one who found the room with the stained glass, she’s in a good number of the photos above, and she is a significantly better mathematician than she lets on in a first conversation.

We’ve been long distance since the last day, which is its own skill and one I’m still bad at. The plan, as of now, is MIT together. Ask me in April how that went.

Going home

I worked out the hours in one of those goodbye letters, because that’s how I handle things I can’t sit inside: eighteen hours a day for forty days, around seven hundred and twenty hours. A normal school year buys me maybe three hundred and sixty with the friends I actually talk to. Six weeks in Ohio came out to roughly two school years of a person, compressed.

The strange part is that the dread never went anywhere, it just changed what it was about. Week one I dreaded the next problem set. Week six I dreaded the drive home. Same feeling, pointed in the opposite direction, and I didn’t notice the switch until I was packing.

I wrote more about that on my blog, because coming home from Ross turned out to be a rehearsal for senior year, when the same thing happened again more slowly to everyone I’d grown up around.

Six weeks and I went from dreading the next problem set to dreading the drive home.
