On this page.Almost everything here started the same way: I finally understood something, and then immediately wanted to explain it to somebody. That impulse has often turned into a lot of pages that are featured here. Writing for a reader is a different problem from solving. A proof only has to be correct; an exposition has to be correct and land for someone who isn’t already convinced. Getting that wrong is often invisible to me but obvious to them, so bear with me. :]

2.

Current Textbooks

ARC · competition mathematics

ARC: AIME Roadmap & Curricula (with Ryan Gong); completed Combinatorics volume (2026-present).

ARC is the book we wanted when we were starting out: a path from the end of the school curriculum to the AIME that treats the reader as someone who wants to understand a technique rather than memorize a list of them. Each chapter builds one tool, shows where it came from, and then hands over problems chosen to fail in instructive ways.

The combinatorics volume is finished at 573 pages, running from counting principles through inclusion–exclusion, expected value, generating functions, and proof technique. Number theory and algebra are in progress, with geometry planned to close the series. It is the project I expect to still be working on when I graduate.

Current Notes

Abstract algebra & number theory · Math Team, 2026–27

An Abstract Algebra & Number Theory Course, written and taught at club meetings.

A course I wrote and now teach, modeled on how Ross does it: start from the order and ring axioms, prove the things everyone assumes, and let the interesting theorems arrive as consequences rather than announcements. These are real lecture notes with problem sets behind them, not slides.

Each lecture ships with a matching homework set, and a few results are posed as prove-or-disprove-and-salvage rather than stated outright, which is the part students find most annoying and learn the most from.

5.

Featured Projects

RC car · with Tony Chen, 2025–26

Semi-Realistic RC Car, designed, printed, and wired from scratch.

The idea was to build the car the way a real one gets designed rather than the way a kit goes together. Steering is a genuine Ackermann linkage, so the inside and outside wheels turn through different angles and all four wheel circles share one turn center; fifteen degrees of positive caster gives the moment arm that makes the wheels self-center. The motors were sized from \( \tau = rF \approx 0.18\ \mathrm{N\,m} \) rather than picked off a shelf, and running one per driven wheel let us delete the axle, differential, and prop shafts.

The chassis is a monocoque modeled on Formula SAE and late-1990s F1, checked each revision with light static-stress studies in Fusion. The electronics were the part that fought back: the Raspberry Pi plan lost to GPIO timing and two ESCs at once, and what finally worked was a Y cable from one receiver into both ESCs with the servo on channel one.

Roller coaster data · with Hunter Faderewski

Kennywood and Cedar Point, two accelerometer studies, both live.

We rode with accelerometers and turned the raw logs into ride profiles: g-forces, airtime, and the shape of every drop. A coaster is a physical system you are allowed to instrument by getting on it, and the data comes back messy enough to need real signal processing before it says anything at all.

Each site puts the quantitative side next to the experience, so the plots sit beside the part where we describe what that particular drop actually felt like.
