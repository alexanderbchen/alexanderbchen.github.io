A proof tells you something is true. A simulation lets you watch it happen, which is a different kind of convincing. Everything below is computed the moment you ask, from randomness nobody picked in advance, so if something looks too good, reroll it and see whether it happens again.

1.

Watch a wave get stuck

Anderson localization · the picture

Put a quantum particle on the middle site of a line of 201 atoms and let go. If every site is identical, it races outward in both directions and never stops spreading. Now give each site a random energy. The particle still tries to leave, but its echoes off all that randomness interfere with one another, and it ends up stuck close to where it started.

time 0.0 spread 0.0 sites
our paper

The red line is a high-water mark: the farthest the wave has ever reached at each site. With no disorder it climbs the whole box. Turn σ up and it stops at a ridge, and the spread underneath levels off instead of following the dashed line a free wave would. The red notch under the slider marks the range of disorder our paper actually studies.

One honest caveat: at very small σ the trap gets wider than the box, so weak disorder can look like none at all here. On an infinite line, any σ above zero traps the wave eventually. That part is a theorem; this is just a picture of it.

2.

Measure how stuck

The paper · Advances in Nonlinear Analysis 15 (2026)

Watching isn’t measuring. Our paper turns “stuck” into a number. The correlator \( Q(k) \) caps how much of the wave can ever show up \( k \) sites away, and on average it decays like \( e^{-\gamma k} \). Nobody hands you \( \gamma \), though. You estimate it by averaging over many random lines, and our Lemma 3.1 says, before you start, how far off that average can be.

samples K = 0 band half-width ε = — your estimate of γ = — ours, from K = 200,000: 0.0252

Press run and you’re redoing our experiment, one random line of 201 sites at a time. The shaded band is Lemma 3.1 at 95% confidence: with that probability, the true curve sits inside it at every distance at once. The dashed red line is the steepest exponential that never dips below your curve, and its slope is your estimate of \( \gamma \). The little mark on the right edge is where ours landed.

The band shrinks like \( 1/\sqrt{K} \), so halving it costs four times the samples. That’s why every entry in our tables took \( K = 200{,}000 \) of them. The readout keeps track of how long your browser would need to catch up.

3.

Drag α past 0.585

Nesbitt, generalized · solo manuscript

Nesbitt’s inequality says \( \frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b}\ge\frac{3}{2} \) for positive \( a, b, c \), with equality at \( a=b=c \). Raise each term to a power \( \alpha \) and the question gets interesting: where does the minimum go? Every point in the triangle is a choice of \( (a,b,c) \) with \( a+b+c=1 \), shaded by the value of \( \sum \big(\tfrac{a}{b+c}\big)^{\alpha} \). Darker means lower, and the red rings mark the bottom.

center 3·2−α = 1.5000 edge midpoints = 2 minimum at the center

At \( \alpha = 1 \) the bottom of the bowl sits dead center. Slide \( \alpha \) down and the center climbs, \( 3\cdot 2^{-\alpha} \), while the midpoints of the edges stay pinned at exactly 2. The two meet when \( 3\cdot 2^{-\alpha}=2 \), at \( \alpha^\star=\log_2\tfrac{3}{2}\approx 0.585 \). Below that, no point inside the triangle is the minimum anymore. The infimum moves out to the edges, where two variables are equal and the third has shrunk to nothing: a value you can get as close to as you like and never actually reach.

That jump is the phase transition, and it’s the reason the sharp constant needs two formulas instead of one.
